Starter School · No. 07

What to feed it.

Rye, whole wheat, white, or a blend. The flour in the jar changes how fast your starter moves, how sour it gets, and how easy it is to read — and rye is the emergency lever worth keeping in the cupboard.

§ 01 — what changes Bran carries everything.

Whole grain flours keep the bran and germ, and that's where most of the useful material is: more resident microbes, more minerals, and far more enzyme activity converting starch into the simple sugars a culture can eat immediately. White flour is mostly endosperm — clean starch, less of everything else.

So a starter fed whole grain is faster, more vigorous, and more sour. Fed white, it's slower, milder, and — the underrated part — much easier to read. A white starter in a clear jar shows its rise unmistakably; a dark rye one hides it.

Rye is the extreme of this. It has the highest enzyme activity of the common flours, which is why it's the standard rescue for a sluggish culture.

§ 02 — the options Four, and a recommendation.
FlourBehaviourGood for
White breadSlower, mild, very easy to readLearning; mild bread; clear visual rise
Whole wheatFaster, tangier, harder to readA robust everyday culture
RyeFastest, most sour, sticky, hides its riseRescues; rye bread; maximum activity
75% white / 25% wholeBrisk but readableThe default worth starting from

The blend is the recommendation for most people: enough whole grain for real vigour, enough white that you can still see what's happening in the jar. Once you can read your starter confidently, move it wherever you like.

The one to avoid Bleached all-purpose. The bleaching process is hard on the microbial life you're trying to cultivate, and it's the one flour that reliably makes a starter sluggish for no upside. Unbleached AP is fine.
§ 03 — the rescue lever A spoonful of rye.

Keep a small bag of rye even if you never bake with it. When a starter is slow — after the fridge, after neglect, in a cold kitchen, or during the quiet days of building a new one — swapping 20–30% of the feed to rye is the fastest single intervention available.

It usually shows within a feed or two. Once activity is back, switch to whatever you were on. You don't have to commit to a rye starter to use rye as a tool.

§ 04 — switching Consistency beats optimisation.

Changing flour takes two or three feeds to settle. The culture rebalances toward whatever the new food favours, and the first feed after a switch is often unrepresentative — faster or slower than it'll end up.

Which leads to the rule that matters more than the choice itself: pick one and hold it. A starter fed the same thing on the same rhythm becomes predictable, and predictability is the only property you actually need. A starter fed whatever's open — rye Monday, AP Wednesday, whole wheat Friday — never settles into a rhythm you can plan around.

The flour matters less than the consistency. That's true of most of sourdough.

Notes from the counter

There's a persistent idea that a "better" flour makes a better starter, usually pointing at expensive stone-ground organic whole grain. It'll make a vigorous one. It will not make a better loaf than a well-maintained white starter, and it will be harder to read while you're learning.

What actually improves a starter is being fed on schedule at a sensible ratio in a room that isn't freezing. Everything on this page is a second-order adjustment on top of that.

Keep the rye though. It's two dollars and it has got more starters through a bad week than any other single thing in the cupboard.
