01 — the bare necessities The Essentials.

This is the minimum viable sourdough setup. Everything here, nothing more. If you have these, you can bake a real loaf.

start here
⚖️

Digital Kitchen Scale

Sourdough is baked by weight, not cups. This is non-negotiable. The OXO 11-lb pull-out display model is the near-universal pick — even America's Test Kitchen lands on it — because the display slides out from under the platform so you can read it with a big bowl in the way. The Escali Primo is the budget pick if you want simple and accurate for less than half the price.

Look for: 1g precision, at least 5kg capacity, tare function.
OXO (the pick) → Escali Primo (budget) →
🫙

Starter Jar

A wide-mouth glass jar with straight sides so you can watch the rise. The Weck 743 (~25oz) is the one everyone uses — wide mouth, straight sides, easy to mark with a rubber band. A loose cloth cover works better than a tight lid.

Straight sides matter: you can't gauge rise in a tapered jar.

Bench Scraper

Cheap, indispensable. Shapes the dough, cleans the counter, divides loaves, scrapes everything. Get a metal one with a comfortable handle. You will use this every single bake.

Under $15. No reason to spend more.

Banneton (Proofing Basket)

The rattan basket that gives sourdough its spiral pattern. A 9" round works for a standard 900g loaf. Get one with a linen liner — it releases more cleanly than bare rattan and is easier to maintain.

Rice flour in the basket = no sticking. Regular flour will not cut it.

🍚

Banneton Basket Liners

You could use rice flour, but why do that? Reusable liners are the way to go. There very cost effective and come in large quanities. It will take a while to run out and they store nicely.

Keep one in your bannetons. It will be ready to go when you reach for it.

Bread Lame (Scoring Tool)

A curved razor blade on a handle. Scoring the dough before it goes in the oven controls where it opens — and it's how you get that ear. The curved-blade lames are easier to use than straight ones for beginners.

Replacement blades exist. Stock up.
View on Amazon →
the big one
🫕

Dutch Oven (5–5.5qt)

The most important piece of equipment you'll buy. The lid traps steam from the dough in the first 20 minutes — that's what gives sourdough its oven spring and crust. Most guides point you at a deep round Dutch oven, but the smarter first buy is the Lodge Combo Cooker: the shallow skillet half becomes your base, so you load dough onto a flat surface instead of dropping it down into a screaming-hot deep pot. America's Test Kitchen named it their Best Buy for exactly this reason, and it's around $40.

If you'd rather have a traditional round pot, a 5–5.5qt enameled Dutch oven works beautifully too. Le Creuset is the forever pot (~$380); the Challenger Bread Pan is the dedicated splurge serious bakers covet. All of them make excellent bread — the Lodge will just outlast your kitchen.

Must be oven-safe to 500°F. On round pots, check the knob — some plastic ones aren't rated that high.
Lodge Combo Cooker (the pick) → Lodge round 5qt → Le Creuset (splurge) →
02 — taking it further Level Up.

You've got the basics down. Oso is reliable. Your loaves are decent. Here's what takes it from decent to genuinely good.

🌡️

Instant-Read Thermometer

Temperature is everything in sourdough — your kitchen temp, your water temp, and your dough temp all affect fermentation speed. A good instant-read (Thermapen or Thermopop) also tells you when the inside of your loaf hits 208°F and is actually done.

Doubles for every other cooking task. Worth owning.
View on Amazon →
🌀

Dough Whisk (Danish Whisk)

Looks like something from a different dimension. Works better than a spoon, easier to clean than a stand mixer for wet sourdough dough. The open coil incorporates flour without overworking the gluten.

Under $15 and genuinely useful. One of those things you mock until you use it.

Bulk Fermentation Tub

A clear, straight-sided container lets you watch dough volume increase during bulk. A 4–6qt tub with measurement markings removes a lot of guesswork about when bulk is actually done.

Cambro and Vollrath make the ones you'll see in every baker's kitchen.

Proofing Box (Brod & Taylor)

The expensive answer to "why does my starter behave differently in winter." A proofing box holds a consistent temperature so your fermentation is predictable regardless of your kitchen. The Brod & Taylor foldable model is the one the serious home baker community has converged on — it also doubles for yogurt, tempering chocolate, and proofing yeasted breads.

Not essential. But once you use one, you'll never want to be without it. Temperature control is a legitimate cheat code.

03 — beyond the dutch oven Open Baking.

Open baking means ditching the Dutch oven lid and baking directly on a steel or stone with oven steam. More control, better crust on multiple loaves, and you can bake two or four at a time instead of one. It's how bakeries do it.

the foundation
🔲

Baking Steel

A thick steel slab that conducts heat far faster than ceramic. Preheat it for 45–60 minutes at your oven's max temp, and your bread gets a blast of bottom heat that stone can't match — better oven spring, deeper crust, more open crumb. The Baking Steel Original (⅜" thick) works for single loaves. The Plus (½" thick, 15"×20") fits four loaves at once.

Also the best pizza tool you'll ever own. Pulls double duty endlessly.
Large Baking Steel → Small Baking Steel →
🍕

Pizza Peel (Transfer Board)

How you get a shaped loaf onto a screaming-hot surface without losing its structure. A wooden peel works well for bread since dough slides more easily off wood than metal. Enough room to maneuver matters.

Perforated metal is better for pizza. Solid wood is better for bread.
View on Amazon →
🌋

Lava Rocks + Cast Iron Pan (Steam)

Open baking requires you to generate your own steam. The classic home setup: a cast iron skillet filled with lava rocks on the bottom rack, preheated until glowing, then doused with boiling water as the bread goes in. Violent and effective. Lava rocks hold heat and throw steam faster than an empty pan, and the cast iron survives the thermal shock that would crack a regular pan.

A turkey baster of boiling water reaches the pan without burning yourself on the oven door.
Lava Rocks → Cast Iron Pan →
🚿

Food-Safe Spray Bottle

A backup steam method or supplement: mist the oven walls and dough surface as you load. Works well alongside the lava rock setup. Also useful for keeping dough from drying out during bulk in dry kitchens.

Keep a dedicated one for the kitchen. Label it.
View on Amazon →
🥄

Long-Handle Oven Gloves

Regular mitts don't cut it when you're reaching into a 500°F oven past a boiling steam setup. Long silicone gloves that go past the wrist are worth having for open baking — and honestly for anything Dutch oven related too.

The cheap ones work fine. You don't need the fancy pair.

04 — the stuff you use up Consumables.

The stuff you actually use up. Flour choices matter more than most equipment choices. Here's what's worth buying.

the workhorse
🌾

King Arthur Bread Flour

High-protein (12.7%), unbleached, consistent batch to batch. This is the flour most serious home sourdough bakers use as their baseline — widely available, reliably strong, gives you a loaf that holds its shape. The 10lb bag makes sense if you're baking weekly. (More on why protein matters in the Flour Compendium.)

The organic version has slightly lower protein but is worth it if you care about sourcing.
5lb Bag → 10lb Bag →
🌾

Bob's Red Mill Artisan Bread Flour

The other community favorite. Slightly different character than King Arthur — some bakers prefer the flavor, others find KA more reliable. Try both. Milled from high-protein North American wheat, unbleached, unbromated.

Also great for baguettes, focaccia, and pizza.
View on Amazon →
🌿

Whole Wheat Flour

Adding 10–20% whole wheat adds flavor, speeds fermentation (the bran feeds the starter), and gives the crust more color. King Arthur's is finely milled and doesn't affect structure as aggressively as coarser grinds. (See the whole wheat entry for blending notes.)

Bob's Red Mill stone-ground WW is coarser and more assertive — great for heartier loaves.
View on Amazon →
for millers
⚙️

Grain Mill

If you want to mill your own flour, this is the machine. The Mockmill (100 or 200) is the stone mill the home sourdough community has largely converged on — it grinds fine enough for bread, adjusts on the fly, and mills straight into your bowl. The Nutrimill Classic is the budget-friendly impact-mill alternative if you're milling large batches and don't mind the noise.

Already have a stand mixer? The KitchenAid grain mill attachment is the cheapest way to dip a toe in before committing to a dedicated machine.

Fresh-milled flour goes rancid faster — mill what you'll use within a week or two, and store the rest cold.
Mockmill (the pick) → Nutrimill (budget) → KitchenAid attachment →
🌰

Wheat Berries (for home milling)

If you're milling your own — and it's worth trying — hard white wheat berries are the most versatile starting point. Milder flavor than hard red, good protein, plays well with sourdough. Hard red berries are more assertive and whole-grain forward. Both are best bought in bulk and stored cool and dry.

You'll need a grain mill (above) to use these. Whole berries keep for years; flour does not.
Hard White → Hard Red →
🧂

Fine Sea Salt

Table salt has iodine, which inhibits the wild yeast in your starter. Fine sea salt (no iodine) is what you want. Diamond Crystal kosher works too — but it's less dense than Morton's, so don't substitute by weight without adjusting.

The amount matters more than the brand. Weigh it.

05 — the only three you need Books.

Three books. That's it. Everything else is noise.

start here

Artisan Sourdough Made Simple

Emilie Raffa

The clearest beginner sourdough book that exists. No unnecessary complexity, no intimidating formulas — just straightforward instruction and genuinely useful recipe variations once you've got the basic loaf down. This is the one to hand someone who just asked you how to start.

View on Amazon →

Tartine Bread

Chad Robertson

The book that started the modern sourdough obsession. Dense, demanding, written by someone who treats bread the way other people treat religion. Don't start here. Come here after you've baked a few dozen loaves and want to understand what's actually happening.

The recipes assume things they don't say out loud. Treat it as a philosophy, not a manual.
View on Amazon →

The Perfect Loaf

Maurizio Leo

The systematic, methodical counterpart to Tartine's intuitive approach. Maurizio Leo brings a software engineer's brain to bread — everything is explained, measured, and testable. The chapters on flour and fermentation are worth the cover price alone.

View on Amazon →
