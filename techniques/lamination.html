Technique · No. 08

Lamination.

Stretch the whole dough into one thin sheet across the counter, then fold it back up. One dramatic move that replaces two ordinary ones — and the only sane way to get inclusions evenly through a loaf.

home/ techniques/ lamination
Takes~3 min
When1st hour
Replaces1–2 folds
Best forInclusions
DifficultyModerate
NeedsWet counter
§ 01 — what it's for Two jobs. One is unique.

Strength, quickly. Stretching the entire dough into a thin sheet aligns gluten across the whole mass at once, rather than at four edges. One lamination does roughly what two sets of folds would — useful when your schedule is tight.

Inclusions, evenly. This is the job nothing else does well. Trying to fold olives, cheese, walnuts or seeds into a formed dough gives you clumps, torn gluten, and a loaf that's half plain. Spread them across a thin sheet and roll it up, and they end up distributed through every layer.

If you're not adding anything, lamination is optional — a faster route to the same strength. If you are, it's the technique.

§ 02 — how Wet the counter. Genuinely wet.

This is the one place in bread where you want water on the work surface rather than a bare one. A wet counter lets the dough slide and spread rather than gripping and tearing. Flour would do the opposite.

About 45–60 minutes into bulk, tip the dough onto the wet counter. Working from the middle outward with wet hands, stretch it into a thin rectangle — thin enough to see the counter through it in places. Go slowly at the edges; that's where it tears.

Scatter inclusions over the surface now, leaving a clean margin around the edge so the seams still seal. Then fold: left third in, right third over, and roll it up from the far end toward you into a fat parcel. Lift it back into the container seam-side down.

Then leave it alone for 30–45 minutes before the next fold. Lamination is a big intervention and the dough needs to relax.

Small tears are fine A hole or two doesn't matter — it all gets folded back together. What matters is not shredding the sheet into pieces, which happens if you stretch fast or work a dough that's already too far into bulk.
§ 03 — inclusions Two rules, both about water.

Wet things must be dried. Olives, sun-dried tomatoes, roasted garlic, anything brined — pat them properly dry. Surface liquid wrecks the gluten it touches and you get slick, weak patches through the loaf.

Dry things must be soaked. Seeds and grains are thirsty. Added dry they pull water out of the dough — the crumb goes tight and the loaf bakes drier than intended. Soak them for a few hours and drain well first.

Keep total inclusions around 15–20% of flour weight. Past that they start interrupting the gluten network faster than it can hold together, and you get a dense loaf with good flavour and no structure.

Cheese specifically Use a hard cheese in cubes, not grated. Grated melts into the crumb and greases the gluten; cubes survive as pockets. Keep them off the outer edge or they leak, burn on the pot, and weld the loaf down.

Notes from the counter

Lamination photographs incredibly well — a translucent sheet of dough covering an entire counter is the single most impressive-looking thing in home baking, which is most of why it spread.

For a plain loaf, it's a shortcut, not an upgrade. Three sets of folds get you to the same place with less drama and less risk of tearing.

But put anything in the bread and it stops being optional. There's no good way to get a fistful of walnuts evenly through a formed dough, and every attempt ends with a loaf that's plain at one end and a walnut brick at the other. That's the case for learning it.
