Reading the bulk.

The one judgement that decides more about your loaf than everything else combined — and the one every recipe reduces to a number of hours it has no business promising you.

§ 01 — why the clock lies Four hours is not a measurement.

Every recipe says something like "bulk ferment 4–5 hours." That number was true in the kitchen of the person who wrote it, on the day they wrote it, with their starter.

Fermentation speed roughly doubles for every 17°F / 9°C. A dough that finishes bulk in 4 hours at 78°F needs closer to 8 at 68°F. Add in how vigorous your starter is, how much of it you used, what flour you're on, and whether your kitchen drifts overnight — and the honest version of "4–5 hours" is "somewhere between 3 and 9."

So the clock is a prompt to go and look. The dough is the instruction. Learning to read it is the difference between following recipes and baking bread.

§ 02 — what you're looking at Four signals, in order.

1 · Volume. The primary signal, and the only one that's close to measurable. How much has it grown from where it started? Not "has it doubled" — how much. More on the target in § 03.

2 · The edges. Look where the dough meets the side of the container. Early in bulk it slumps away and sits flat. As it ferments it climbs and the edge becomes domed — a convex curve rising to meet the wall. When it goes past its best, that dome sinks back and the edge flattens or dips.

3 · The jiggle. Shake the container gently. Under-fermented dough is dense and barely moves. Ready dough wobbles as one soft mass, like set jelly. Over-fermented dough sloshes — the movement is loose and liquid rather than springy.

4 · The surface. Domed, slightly glossy, with some bubbles visible on top and through the sides. A flat, matte, bubble-free surface means it needs more time. A surface covered in large broken bubbles that are collapsing as you watch means you're late.

§ 03 — how much rise It depends on what comes next.

This is where most advice goes wrong by giving one number. The right target depends entirely on what happens after bulk:

What follows bulkStop bulk atWhy
Overnight cold retard30–50%Fermentation keeps running for hours as the dough cools
Shape and bake same day60–75%Only the short proof left to finish it
High whole grainLower endFerments faster; the gluten degrades sooner
Enriched doughHigher endFat and sugar slow the yeast down

"Doubled" — the number most recipes give — is almost always too far for a dough heading into the fridge. That single mismatch is behind a large share of flat, over-proofed loaves.

This is why the Calibration Loaf says 50% It cold retards overnight, so it stops at 50% and lets the fridge finish the job — step 5.
§ 04 — the aliquot jar Stop guessing at percentages.

Eyeballing "50% risen" in a wide bowl is genuinely hard, because a bowl's cross-section changes with height — a 50% volume increase might only look like a couple of centimetres.

The fix costs nothing. Right after mixing, take a small piece of dough — 30–40 g — and press it into a straight-sided container: a shot glass, a small jar, a narrow tumbler. Mark the level with a rubber band. That sample sits next to the main bowl, at the same temperature, fermenting at the same rate.

Because the sides are straight, height is volume. When the aliquot has risen 50%, so has your dough — and you can read it off precisely instead of squinting into a bowl.

One rule Same temperature, same time, same dough. Put the jar right beside the bowl. A sample sitting somewhere warmer is measuring a different dough.

Notes from the counter

If there's one page on this site I'd want a beginner to read twice, it's this one. Shaping is a motor skill you'll pick up in twenty loaves. Scoring takes ten. This is the one that takes a season, because you're learning to read a continuous process from a handful of ambiguous signals, and you only get one data point per bake.

Which is the actual argument for the Calibration Loaf, and for baking the same thing repeatedly early on. Every variable you hold still is one less thing muddying the signal you're trying to learn.

The aliquot jar feels like cheating and isn't. Professional bakeries do the equivalent with temperature probes and rise charts. Give yourself an instrument rather than relying on judgement you haven't built yet — the judgement comes anyway, faster, once you can see what 50% actually looks like.
