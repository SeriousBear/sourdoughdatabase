Technique · No. 07

Steam, and why the tray of water fails.

Steam buys you about twenty minutes of expansion before the crust sets. A Dutch oven delivers it almost perfectly by accident. Most of the alternatives people try in home ovens don't work, for a reason worth understanding.

§ 01 — what steam does It delays the crust.

In a dry oven, the loaf's surface dries out and sets within a few minutes. Meanwhile the inside is still expanding hard — gas warming, water turning to vapour, yeast making a final burst before the heat kills it. A set crust against an expanding interior means the loaf either tears somewhere ugly or simply stops growing.

Steam keeps the surface moist and flexible for those critical minutes, so the loaf can keep climbing. That's the main job, and it's why steam only matters for roughly the first 20 minutes. After that the interior has set, expansion is over, and steam becomes counterproductive — you want dry heat to build colour and crispness.

The second effect is cosmetic but real: moisture on the surface gelatinises the starch, which is what produces the glossy, blistered crust. That's a byproduct of the same conditions.

§ 02 — the Dutch oven It works because it's small.

A Dutch oven doesn't add steam. It traps the loaf's own. A 900 g dough is roughly a third water, and as it heats it releases plenty of vapour — in a sealed cast iron pot that's a saturated environment within a minute or two.

The reason this works so well is volume. Your oven is maybe 60 litres and vented; the pot is about 5 litres and sealed. The same amount of vapour that would be a faint mist in the oven is a fog in the pot.

Preheat it for 45 minutes with the lid on. The pot needs to be at temperature, not just the oven — cast iron takes far longer than air, and an underheated pot is a common cause of a pale loaf with no spring. Then: 20 minutes covered at 500°F / 260°C, lid off, drop to 450°F / 232°C, 20–25 minutes more until deeply browned and 205–210°F / 96–99°C inside.

Don't peek Lifting the lid in the first 20 minutes dumps the entire steam environment in a second, and it takes minutes to rebuild — by which time the window has closed.
§ 03 — the alternatives Ranked, honestly.
MethodWorks?Why
Dutch oven / combo cookerYesSmall sealed volume, the loaf steams itself
Cloche or clay bakerYesSame principle, same result
Deep tray + inverted bowlMostlyImprovised enclosure. Seal is worse but the idea is right
Preheated pan + boiling waterMarginalA hot mass of metal or lava rock helps. A home oven still vents faster than you can fill it
Cold tray of water on the shelfNoToo little energy to flash to steam quickly. Mostly just sits there
Spraying the oven wallsNoSmall water, big vented space, and each door-open loses more heat than the spray adds
Ice cubes on a trayMarginalBetter than cold water — the pan must be preheated and heavy

The pattern: anything that encloses a small volume works. Anything that tries to humidify a whole domestic oven struggles, because home ovens are designed to vent moisture — that's what stops your roast steaming instead of browning.

No Dutch oven? Cover the loaf with a stainless bowl or a deep roasting tin for the first 20 minutes. It's a worse seal and it works far better than any spray bottle. Gear notes are in The Pantry.

Black on the bottom? Almost always conducted heat from the pot, not oven temperature — burnt bottom.
Still no rise? If preheat and steam are right and the loaf still doesn't climb, the cause is upstream — no oven spring, and usually the bulk.

Notes from the counter

Steam is the step where home bakers spend the most money and get the least return. There's a whole market of gadgets — steam injectors, baking systems, purpose-built pans — competing to solve a problem that a $40 cast iron pot solves completely.

And the thing worth repeating: a Dutch oven isn't adding anything. It's a lid. It works because the loaf was always producing enough steam and your oven was throwing it away.

Once you've got a pot, steam stops being a variable you think about — which is the real value. It moves the problem off your list so you can go back to worrying about fermentation, which is where the loaf is actually won or lost.
