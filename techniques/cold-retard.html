The cold retard.

Put the shaped loaf in the fridge overnight and three separate things improve at once — flavour, scoring, and your schedule. It's the highest-value, lowest-effort move in sourdough, and it's mostly just waiting.

§ 01 — what cold does It doesn't stop. It re-balances.

The instinct is that the fridge pauses fermentation. It doesn't — it slows everything down, but it slows the two populations by different amounts, and that difference is the whole technique.

Yeast activity drops off sharply in the cold. The lactic acid bacteria keep working comparatively better. So over a long cold rest, gas production nearly stops while acid production continues — which means the dough develops flavour without over-inflating.

That's the trade you're making: hours of flavour development for very little extra rise. It's why a retarded loaf tastes complex and tangy in a way a same-day loaf never does, no matter how long you leave it on the counter.

The first hour still counts A shaped loaf takes a while to actually reach fridge temperature — often 1–2 hours for a home fridge and a dense dough. Fermentation runs at close to room-temperature speed for that whole window, which is why bulk should stop at 30–50% when a retard follows.
§ 02 — the other two wins Flavour is only the first.

Scoring. A cold dough is firm. The blade goes through cleanly instead of dragging and tearing a slack, sticky surface. Almost everyone who struggles to score is scoring warm dough — this fixes it without any change in technique. More on scoring.

Your schedule. Without a retard, the loaf is ready when it's ready, which is regularly 11pm. With one, you mix and shape on Saturday evening and bake whenever suits on Sunday. The window is genuinely wide — anywhere from 8 to about 36 hours works, so "when I get up" and "after lunch" are both fine.

That flexibility is what makes sourdough sustainable for people with jobs, and it's the single biggest reason to build the habit early.

§ 03 — how to do it Shaped, covered, straight in.

Shape the loaf, place it seam-side up in a floured banneton or a tea-towel-lined bowl, cover it, and put it in the fridge immediately. Don't rest it on the counter first — that's just extra warm fermentation you haven't accounted for.

Cover it properly. A shower cap, a bag, or a plate on top. An uncovered loaf develops a dry skin overnight that scores badly and bakes into a leathery patch.

The next day, take it straight from the fridge, score it cold, and bake it cold. Don't let it come to room temperature first — you'd lose the firmness that makes it easy to score, and you'd add an hour of unplanned proofing.

§ 04 — bulk or shaped? Shaped, almost always.

You can refrigerate the dough during bulk instead, and some bakers do — it's useful if you get called away mid-bulk, and a cold dough is easier to shape if you're working at very high hydration.

But retarding after shaping is better in almost every ordinary case, because the two side benefits both depend on it. A cold bulk still leaves you shaping and proofing a warm loaf on baking day, which means no firm dough to score and no schedule flexibility — you've kept the flavour and thrown away the rest.

If you need to interrupt bulk, chill it, then bring it back to room temperature and finish bulk before shaping. Don't treat a chilled bulk as a finished one.

Notes from the counter

If you only adopt one thing from this whole library, adopt this. It costs no effort, it improves the bread on three separate axes, and it converts sourdough from a thing that eats a whole day into a thing that fits around one.

The under-appreciated benefit is the last one. Most people who quit sourdough don't quit because the bread was bad — they quit because it demanded a full Saturday and kept them up until midnight. A retard turns that into forty minutes on Saturday evening and a bake whenever you surface on Sunday.

The bread is better too. But the bread being better is the reason people say to do it, and the schedule is the reason they keep doing it.
