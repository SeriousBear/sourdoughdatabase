§ 01 — how this works You bring the ingredients. It brings the arithmetic.

Every other recipe on this site tells you what to weigh. This one asks. Put whatever you're actually using into the bench below — flour, water, starter, salt, and anything else you feel like throwing in — and it writes the rest: the true hydration, the baker's percentages, what your particular combination is going to do to the dough, and a method with the numbers already filled in.

It starts you on the Calibration Loaf, which is the plainest useful loaf we know how to describe. Change one thing or change all of it. Water is just the default liquid — pour beer instead, or half beer, and the numbers move with you.

Have at it. Have fun with it. We are, nominally, trying to make something edible here — and the tool will say so when you wander off that path. It will not stop you. Somebody has to find out what happens with the Dr Pepper, and it is not going to be us.

§ 04 — what your blend will do The part no calculator tells you.

These change as you build. They're about what the dough will feel and do — a number can't tell you that a 30% rye dough is going to stick to everything you own.

They are tips, not physics. The flour absorption figures are measured. What happens when you pour a bottle of Dr Pepper into a levain is not — that one is "here's what usually goes wrong", and the only way to know for certain is to bake it.

§ 05 — the method Ten steps, written for your dough.
Written for what's on the bench This method matches your current ingredients — 450 g white bread flour at 70% hydration. Change something above and this turns red until you rewrite it.

Written for 450 g of flour at 70% hydration, finishing at about 860 g of dough. Times assume a kitchen around 74°F / 23°C — build the clock for your actual room if yours runs cold.

  1. Feed the starter
    Morning · 4–6 hours before you mix

    You need 100 g of starter at peak, so feed at least 120 g to leave yourself a margin and something to keep. A 1:1:1 by weight — equal starter, flour and water — left at room temperature will get you there.

    Peak means roughly doubled, domed on top, smelling yeasty and faintly sweet rather than sharp. Past peak it has already spent its rise and your bulk will crawl. Mark the jar with a rubber band; watch the level, not the clock.

    Don't have a starter yet? Nothing else here works without one. Make one from scratch — flour, water, and about two weeks.
    Because you're at 20% starter
    That's a standard dose and the timings below assume it. Drop to 10% and everything stretches by roughly half again; push past 25% and the bulk will be over before you've finished the folds — feeding and ratios covers the trade.
  2. Mix the flour and water
    2:00 pm · 5 minutes, then 1 hour rest

    Whisk 450 g of flour and 300 g of warm water together until there is no dry flour left anywhere. It will look shaggy and unpromising. That's correct.

    Cover it and leave it. This is the autolyse, and it does real work — the flour hydrates fully and the gluten starts organising itself without you kneading anything.

  3. Add the starter and the salt
    3:00 pm · 3 minutes

    Add 100 g of peaked starter and 10 g of salt straight on top. Wet one hand and squeeze the dough repeatedly between your fingers, folding it over itself, until you can't see any streaks. About three minutes.

    It will feel slack and sticky and slightly wrong. It is supposed to. Don't add flour.

  4. Bulk ferment, with folds
    3:00–7:00 pm · 4 sets in the first 2 hours

    Cover and leave it at room temperature. At 30, 60, 90 and 120 minutes, do one set of stretch and folds, then leave it completely alone. You'll watch it get smoother, puffier, and start holding a dome instead of slumping flat.

  5. Judge the bulk — the one that matters
    Around 7:00 pm · volume, not the clock

    This is the skill. Everything else here is mechanical; this is the part you're actually learning.

    Stop when three things are true: the dough has risen about 50%, the edges where it meets the bowl are domed rather than sunken, and the whole mass jiggles as one soft body when you shake it.

    If it's 7:00 and it hasn't done that, wait. If it got there early, move on. The clock is a suggestion; the dough is the instruction.

    This is the skill Volume, dome, jiggle, and the aliquot-jar trick that turns "50%" from a guess into a measurement — reading the bulk.
  6. Preshape and rest
    7:00 pm · 20 minutes

    Tip the dough onto an unfloured counter. Use a bench knife to drag it toward you in quarter turns until it pulls itself into a taut round. Cover it with the bowl and leave it alone for twenty minutes so the gluten relaxes enough to shape.

  7. Shape
    7:20 pm · 5 minutes

    Flour the top, flip it, and shape into a boule or a bâtard. What you're building is surface tension — a skin tight enough to hold the loaf up while it springs. Seam side up into a floured banneton, cover, and into the fridge.

    Tension, not decoration The three shapes and how to know you've got enough tension — shaping.
  8. Cold retard
    7:30 pm – next morning · 12–16 hours

    Straight into the fridge, uncovered or loosely bagged. This buys flavour, makes the loaf far easier to score, and puts the bake on your schedule instead of the dough's.

    How long is too long? What the cold actually changes, and the ceiling — cold retard.
  9. Score and bake
    Next morning · 20 min covered, 25 min uncovered

    Preheat the Dutch oven to 500°F / 260°C for a full hour. Turn the cold loaf straight out onto parchment, score it decisively in one motion, and get the lid on. Twenty minutes covered, then drop to 450°F / 230°C, lid off, for another twenty-five.

    Take it darker than feels comfortable. Pale crust is the most common self-inflicted wound in sourdough.

    Steam is the whole trick Why covered-then-uncovered works, and the alternatives if you have no Dutch oven — steam and the bake.
  10. Cool — properly
    At least 2 hours

    On a rack, not a board. The loaf is still cooking as it cools and the crumb is still setting. Cutting hot bread gums it, and you will blame the recipe.

Notes from the counter

The Calibration Loaf exists to hold everything still so you can learn to read one thing. This page is the opposite, and you should probably bake that one first.

But holding things still forever is its own trap. At some point the useful move is to change one variable on purpose and find out what it does — and the reason people don't is that changing the flour means redoing the water, and nobody wants to do arithmetic standing up.

So that's what this is for. Not to design a perfect loaf. To make the experiment cheap enough that you actually run it.
